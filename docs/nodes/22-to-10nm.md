# 22 nm to 10 nm

<p class="huv-lead">Chapter 2 of 5 — Between 2011 and 2019 two things happened at once: the transistor went three-dimensional, and the tightest pitches on the chip fell below the roughly 76–80 nm that a single ArF-immersion exposure can print. EUV was not ready for production, so these generations were built by splitting patterns across several exposures and by growing lines as spacers on sacrificial mandrels. 2011–2019: lithography stopped being one exposure per layer and became a small process flow of its own.</p>

Intel values are from its papers and fact sheets. Foundry values are ranges across company
disclosures (TSMC's IEDM papers, Samsung's VLSI papers) and teardown analyses (Chipworks,
Siliconics, IC Knowledge); they differ by a few nanometres between sources. Fin pitches:
Intel 60, 42 and 34 nm; TSMC 48 and ≈ 33–36 nm; Samsung ≈ 49 and ≈ 42 nm (teardowns). For
Samsung 14LPE, TechInsights' teardown of the Exynos 7420 measured a 49 nm fin pitch and a
67 nm minimum metal pitch; other summaries quote 64 nm for the metal pitch.

In k₁ terms (see [lithography arithmetic](litho-math.md)): 64 nm pitch on a 1.35-NA
immersion scanner would need k₁ = 0.22, 52 nm would need 0.18 and 36 nm would need 0.13, all
below the 0.25 floor. Intel's 22 nm, with 80 nm metal at k₁ = 0.28, still printed its tightest
metal in a single exposure; every leading-edge process after it needed multipatterning (or,
later, EUV) for that layer.

## The FinFET arrives

Intel announced its 22 nm "3-D Tri-Gate" transistors on 4 May 2011, with production readiness
promised for the end of that year, and the first Ivy Bridge processors went on sale in April
2012. Intel claimed 37 % more performance at low voltage than its 32 nm planar transistors, or
less than half the power at the same performance. Its VLSI 2012 paper describes "fully-depleted
tri-gate transistors, self-aligned contacts and high density MIM capacitors" at a 60 nm fin
pitch, 90 nm contacted gate pitch and 80 nm minimum metal pitch, and notes that "193nm
immersion lithography is used extensively", including "two layers with 80nm minimum pitch using
cost-effective single patterning".

The foundries followed one generation later. TSMC started 16 nm FinFET risk production in
November 2013 and its 16FF+ entered volume production in July 2015; Samsung began mass
production of its first FinFET application processor in January 2015. Wrapping the gate over
three sides of a thin fin restored the electrostatic control that planar transistors had lost
(see the [transistor figure](index.md#from-planar-to-cfet)); it also put the transistor on a
fixed grid of fins, so a device's width now came in whole fins.

## Pitch splitting: LELE

The most direct fix is to deal a dense pattern out to two masks, alternately, like cards.
Each mask carries every other line, so it only has to print twice the final pitch; "two
separate lithography and etch steps are performed to define a single layer", litho, etch,
litho, etch (LELE), and the two etched patterns interleave.

<figure markdown="span">

![Two rows of interleaved lines labelled A and B. With perfect overlay the spaces between A and B lines are equal; with an overlay error delta on mask B the spaces alternate between s minus delta and s plus delta.](../assets/images/nodes/lele-overlay-light.svg#gh-light-mode-only)
![Two rows of interleaved lines labelled A and B. With perfect overlay the spaces between A and B lines are equal; with an overlay error delta on mask B the spaces alternate between s minus delta and s plus delta.](../assets/images/nodes/lele-overlay-dark.svg#gh-dark-mode-only)

<figcaption><strong>LELE double patterning.</strong> Each exposure prints twice the final pitch, but the second mask's overlay error goes straight into the spaces. <em>Model: multiple patterning (LELE) 🔶 (two exposures, sub-pixel overlay).</em></figcaption>
</figure>

LELE doubles the k₁ of each exposure (64 nm final pitch means 128 nm per mask, k₁ = 0.45 on
immersion) at three costs:

- **Overlay becomes CD.** Any misplacement of mask B relative to mask A turns one space into
  $s - \delta$ and its neighbour into $s + \delta$. As Chris Mack summed it up, "LELE has very
  tight restrictions on overlay. Self-aligned processes are not nearly as critical from an
  overlay perspective."
- **Layouts must be "colourable".** The drawn layout is decomposed into two colours, one per
  mask, and every pair of features closer than the single-exposure limit has to land on
  different masks. Three features that are all too close to each other (an odd cycle) cannot be
  split in two; design rules have to forbid them, or a third colour is added, since "having the
  third color in TP helps overcome the odd cycle problem". Samsung said its 10 nm process used
  triple patterning "to allow bi-directional routing", although a Siliconics teardown of a
  Snapdragon 835 describes its tightest metal as LELE; its 8/7 nm generation is reported to
  have quadruple-patterned its 44 nm-pitch metal.
- **Every pass is a full litho and etch sequence,** with its own CD variation and yield loss.

### Try it in highuvlith

Why one immersion exposure cannot print 64 nm pitch, and one LELE mask can: image a 64 nm
pitch grating in one exposure on a 1.35-NA water-immersion system, then one LELE mask (every
other line, so 128 nm pitch). With σ = 0.9 the single exposure cannot capture a first
diffraction order at 64 nm pitch: $\lambda/p = 193/64 \approx 3.0$ exceeds
$\mathrm{NA} (1+\sigma) = 1.35 \times 1.9 \approx 2.6$, so its contrast is zero.

<!-- verify-example -->
```python
import highuvlith as huv

    mask = huv.MaskConfig.line_space(32.0, pitch)               # 32 nm lines
```

## Spacers: SADP and SAQP

The alternative is to let deposition, not lithography, define the final lines. In **SADP**,
one exposure (the mandrel mask) prints sacrificial mandrels at pitch $P$. A conformal film of
thickness $t$ is deposited over them, an anisotropic etch removes it from horizontal surfaces,
and the mandrels are stripped. What remains are two spacer lines per mandrel, at pitch $P/2$,
with a width set by the deposited thickness rather than by lithography.

<figcaption><strong>Self-aligned double patterning, step by step.</strong> Ideal conformal spacers; no deposition or etch physics. <em>Model: multiple patterning (SADP/SAQP) 🔶 (geometric).</em></figcaption>
</figure>

<figure markdown="span">

![Five cross-section steps of self-aligned double patterning: mandrels at pitch P; conformal spacer film; etch-back leaves spacers on the mandrel sidewalls; mandrel pull leaves two spacer lines per mandrel at pitch P/2; transfer into the target layer.](../assets/images/nodes/sadp-flow-light.svg#gh-light-mode-only)
![Five cross-section steps of self-aligned double patterning: mandrels at pitch P; conformal spacer film; etch-back leaves spacers on the mandrel sidewalls; mandrel pull leaves two spacer lines per mandrel at pitch P/2; transfer into the target layer.](../assets/images/nodes/sadp-flow-dark.svg#gh-dark-mode-only)

<figcaption><strong>Self-aligned double patterning.</strong> One lithography step prints mandrels at pitch P; a conformal spacer, an etch-back and a mandrel pull leave lines at P/2. <em>Model: multiple patterning (SADP/SAQP) 🔶 (geometric).</em></figcaption>
</figure>

Because both lines of a pair grow on the two sidewalls of the same mandrel, "the pitch split is
achieved using the spacers, instead of aligning one mask to another": the pattern is
*self-aligned*, and the hardest process problem moves to "controlling the etch bias on the
spacers". Intel's 14 nm process paper lists "self-aligned double patterning" among its headline
features, used for the fins, the gates and the minimum-pitch metal; Intel's own fact sheet
claimed "a significant density and yield advantage versus the industry litho-etch-litho-etch
(LELE) approach".

**SAQP** repeats the trick: the first spacers become the mandrels for a second spacer, and
the final pitch is $P/4$.

<figure markdown="span">

![Four cross-section steps of self-aligned quadruple patterning: mandrels at pitch P; first spacers at P/2 after the mandrel pull; second spacers formed on the first spacers; first spacers removed, leaving lines at P/4.](../assets/images/nodes/saqp-flow-light.svg#gh-light-mode-only)
![Four cross-section steps of self-aligned quadruple patterning: mandrels at pitch P; first spacers at P/2 after the mandrel pull; second spacers formed on the first spacers; first spacers removed, leaving lines at P/4.](../assets/images/nodes/saqp-flow-dark.svg#gh-dark-mode-only)

<figcaption><strong>Self-aligned quadruple patterning.</strong> The spacer step repeated on the first spacers divides the lithographic pitch by four. <em>Model: multiple patterning (SADP/SAQP) 🔶 (geometric).</em></figcaption>
</figure>

With about 76 nm as the single-exposure immersion limit, SAQP can in principle reach about
19 nm pitch. Intel introduced what it called the "world's first Self-Aligned Quad Patterning"
with its 10 nm process: a teardown analysis of Intel's IEDM 2017 paper summarised the rule as
"any pitch 40 nm or below needs quad-patterning (or LELELE), so the fins, M0 and M1 use SAQP",
with SADP for the next four metal levels. Relative to SADP, SAQP adds four process steps (one
deposition and three etches) but no extra lithography. SAQP also became the standard way to
pattern fins at 7 nm and 5 nm, long after EUV arrived for other layers.

Two variants matter for layout. In the **spacer-is-line** tone the spacers become the metal
lines; in the **spacer-is-dielectric** tone they become the gaps between lines, and metal
fills everything else. The second tone lets the mandrel mask shape some line ends directly and
needs fewer cuts.

## Cut and block masks

Spacer schemes print only regular gratings. The circuit is carved out of them afterwards: a
**cut** (or **block**) mask removes line segments where the layout needs line ends, and a via
mask connects the survivors to the level above.

<figure markdown="span">

![Three top views: a regular spacer grating; cut masks removing line segments and via masks connecting lines to the level above; a cut shifted by a few nanometres that clips the neighbouring line and leaves a sliver, with the edge-placement error dimensioned.](../assets/images/nodes/cut-mask-light.svg#gh-light-mode-only)
![Three top views: a regular spacer grating; cut masks removing line segments and via masks connecting lines to the level above; a cut shifted by a few nanometres that clips the neighbouring line and leaves a sliver, with the edge-placement error dimensioned.](../assets/images/nodes/cut-mask-dark.svg#gh-dark-mode-only)

<figcaption><strong>Cut, block and via masks carve the circuit out of a spacer grating.</strong> A cut that lands a few nanometres off clips the neighbouring line or leaves a sliver: cut overlay is part of the edge-placement budget. <em>Schematic, not to scale.</em></figcaption>
</figure>

Intel first used a separate line-cut mask at 45 nm, for its 160 nm gate pitch. By 10 nm the
cuts and vias were among the hardest exposures on the chip: small, two-dimensional, and placed
relative to lines that were themselves only 18 nm wide. A cut that lands a few nanometres off
can clip a neighbouring line or leave a sliver behind, so cut overlay became part of the
edge-placement budget. The block mask pushes a layer "potentially to the point of only allowing
unidirectional routing", and the tightest levels moved increasingly to one-directional
("1-D gridded") layouts, which spacer patterning and cutting handle well.

## Pitch walk

Self-alignment removes overlay from line *pairs*, but not every error. Any mask misalignment,
or any error in the CD of the pre-patterned mandrels, makes the pitch between neighbouring
features alternate; this is called pitch walking. In SADP the two kinds of space in the
final grating are made differently: the **core** space is where a mandrel used to be (width
$W$, set by lithography and trim), and the **gap** space is what is left between spacers of
neighbouring mandrels ($P - W - 2t$). The two are equal only when $W = P/2 - t$.

<figure markdown="span">

![Three rows of SADP spacer lines. Nominal: core and gap spaces equal. Mandrel CD larger by delta: core spaces widen by delta and gap spaces shrink by delta. Spacer thicker by delta: lines widen and gap spaces shrink by two delta while core spaces stay the same.](../assets/images/nodes/pitch-walk-light.svg#gh-light-mode-only)
![Three rows of SADP spacer lines. Nominal: core and gap spaces equal. Mandrel CD larger by delta: core spaces widen by delta and gap spaces shrink by delta. Spacer thicker by delta: lines widen and gap spaces shrink by two delta while core spaces stay the same.](../assets/images/nodes/pitch-walk-dark.svg#gh-dark-mode-only)

<figcaption><strong>Pitch walk.</strong> A mandrel CD error widens the core spaces and narrows the gaps by the same amount; a spacer-thickness error narrows only the gaps, by twice the error. <em>Model: multiple patterning (SADP/SAQP) 🔶 (geometric).</em></figcaption>
</figure>

A mandrel CD error $\Delta$ widens every core space by $\Delta$ and narrows every gap space by
$\Delta$, so the line-to-line pitch alternates between $P/2 + \Delta$ and $P/2 - \Delta$. The
difference between neighbouring pitches, the **pitch walk**, is

```math
\text{walk}_\text{SADP} = \left| 2W + 2t - P \right| .
```

A spacer that is too thick leaves the core spaces alone and eats into the gaps twice. SAQP has
more ways to walk: its final spaces are set by different combinations of the mandrel CD and
two spacer thicknesses, and if these "don't match, there are unwanted pitch walking and
variability issues". Pitch walk feeds straight into CD and edge-placement errors of the lines
that the cut masks later carve up.

### Try it in highuvlith

`api.sadp(P, W, t)` and `api.saqp(P, W, t1, t2)` build the spacer geometry over one
mandrel period. With a 128 nm mandrel pitch and 32 nm spacers, SADP gives a uniform 64 nm
pitch at W = 32 nm; SAQP gives a uniform 32 nm pitch at W = 48 nm, t₁ = t₂ = 16 nm. Grow
the mandrel and watch the spaces split: for SADP the walk is $2W + 2t - P$, so 4 nm at
W = 34 nm.

<!-- verify-example -->
```python
from highuvlith import api

for w in (32.0, 34.0, 36.0):                                # mandrel CD (nm)
    r = api.sadp(128.0, w, 32.0)
    print(f"SADP  W={w:.0f}: spaces {[round(s, 1) for s in r.spaces_nm]}  walk {r.pitch_walk_nm:.1f} nm")
for w in (48.0, 50.0):
    r = api.saqp(128.0, w, 16.0, 16.0)
    print(f"SAQP  W={w:.0f}: spaces {[round(s, 1) for s in r.spaces_nm]}  walk {r.pitch_walk_nm:.1f} nm")
```

    ```text
    SADP  W=34: spaces [34.0, 30.0]  walk 4.0 nm
    SADP  W=36: spaces [36.0, 28.0]  walk 8.0 nm
    SAQP  W=48: spaces [16.0, 16.0, 16.0, 16.0]  walk 0.0 nm
    ```

## Overlay budgets

Every multipatterning scheme spends overlay somewhere: LELE on the line spaces themselves,
SADP/SAQP on the cut, block and via masks that must land on the spacer-defined grid. Scanner
overlay improved release after release for exactly this reason (ASML specifications for its
1.35-NA immersion platform):

<!-- huv-table: stack -->

| Scanner | First shipped | Dedicated-chuck overlay | Matched-machine overlay |
|---|---|---|---|
| NXT:1950i | 2009 | ≤ 2.5 nm | ≤ 5.5 nm |
| NXT:1970Ci | c. 2013 (in the field by early 2014) | ≤ 2.0 nm | ≤ 3.5 nm |
| NXT:1980Di | 2015 | ≤ 1.6 nm | ≤ 2.5 nm |
| NXT:2000i | c. 2018 | ≤ 1.4 nm | ≤ 2.0 nm |

On-product requirements tightened in step: an ASML roadmap presented at SPIE in 2014 listed
on-product overlay needs of 6.5 nm for 2013–14, 5 to 3.5 nm for 2014–15 and 3.5 to 2.5 nm for
2016–18. Later roadmaps budget overlay at about 20 % of the minimum half-pitch (2.4 nm at a
12 nm half-pitch in the IRDS 2022 tables). Hence the industry-wide push toward self-aligned
vias and self-aligned blocks.

## What it cost

Multipatterning worked, but it multiplied steps. A layer that once needed one exposure and
one etch now needed two to four exposures, several depositions and etches, and extra cut
masks, each adding cycle time, cost and a chance for defects. Intel noted in 2014 that "wafer
cost is increasing due to added masking steps" (while cost per transistor still fell), and in
2017 that "multi-pass patterning adds to the cost of lithography"; a GlobalFoundries engineer
put it more bluntly: "The mask count is going through the roof." The IRDS summarises the
outcome: "due to the rising costs and process complexity of multiple patterning, EUV is used
as a remedy to pattern tight ground rules in fewer process steps." That is the story of the
[next page](7-and-5nm.md).

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Case study: Intel's 10 nm, the most aggressive pitches of the era and the slip that followed</summary>

Intel's 10 nm combined the most aggressive pitches of the era (54 nm gate, 36 nm metal)
with SAQP, contact-over-active-gate and cobalt interconnects in its lowest layers. It
slipped badly: in April 2018 Intel said it was shipping only "low-volume 10 nm product"
and expected volume production in 2019, and the first high-volume 10 nm processors (Ice
Lake) launched in August 2019. Intel's then-CEO said "we think we bit off a little too
much in this case"; his successor later called the goals "more and more aggressive" at a
time "when it was getting harder and harder". In 2021 Intel renamed the improved
"10nm Enhanced SuperFin" process "Intel 7".

</details>

## Key takeaways

- FinFETs arrived at Intel's 22 nm (2011–2012) and at the foundries' 16/14 nm (2014–2015).
- Intel's 22 nm (80 nm metal) still printed its tightest metal in one immersion exposure;
  below about 76–80 nm pitch, LELE, triple patterning, SADP and SAQP filled the gap until EUV.
- Spacer patterning makes lines self-aligned but prints only gratings; cut, block and via
  masks then carry the hardest lithography and the overlay budget.
- LELE turns overlay error into space-CD error one-for-one; SADP/SAQP turn mandrel CD and
  spacer thickness errors into pitch walk.
- The cost in masks, steps and cycle time is what made EUV worth its development cost.
